Neural Networks & Responsible AI

Inside a Neural Network — From Predictions to Responsible AI

An accessible look at neural-network layers, training, personalization, bias, and privacy, adapted from my research on neural networks and GDPR.

By Alysha Pursley

Neural networks are often described as systems that learn patterns from data. That description is accurate, but it leaves out the questions I find most interesting: what actually changes when a network learns, what makes its predictions useful, and what responsibilities come with using those predictions?

In my research on neural-network-powered personalization and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), I explored those questions through a hypothetical social media platform. The example is different from my Treasure Hunt deep Q-learning project, but the underlying idea is related: a model transforms numerical inputs into predictions, and training changes how those predictions are made.

This article draws on that white paper and my neural-network fundamentals writing. It explains the technology first, then considers what happens when the same capabilities are used to make decisions about people.

What a Neural Network Actually Does

A neural network is a mathematical model made of connected processing units arranged in layers. The biological-neuron analogy is a useful starting point, but artificial neurons do not think or operate like cells in a brain. They perform calculations.

An input layer receives numerical features. In a recommendation system, those features might represent clicks, time spent viewing a post, or patterns of interaction. In a maze-learning system, the input might instead describe the current environment.

Hidden layers transform the input. Each unit combines incoming values using learned weights and a bias, then applies an activation function. Functions such as ReLU allow a network to represent relationships that a single linear calculation could not capture.

An output layer produces values that the application can interpret. A personalization model might score content for relevance; a classification model might estimate the likelihood of a category; a Q-network estimates the value of possible actions. The meaning of the output depends on the task, not on the mere presence of a neural network.

How Training Turns Calculations Into Predictions

Before training, the network's weights do not encode a useful solution to the task. Training repeatedly compares model outputs with a learning objective and updates the weights to reduce error or improve estimated value.

In supervised learning, the objective often compares a prediction with a known target. Backpropagation calculates how changes to weights affect the loss, and an optimizer uses that information to update the network. This is the training process I described in my neural-network research.

Deep Q-learning uses a different target: it estimates how valuable an action is in a state, based on rewards and expected future rewards. The network still learns through weight updates, but the feedback comes from interaction with an environment rather than a straightforward list of correct labels.

That distinction is important in my portfolio. My original Python Treasure Hunt notebook explores deep Q-learning with a neural network and experience replay. The playable browser opponent uses pathfinding, not the trained network. The two implementations should not be described as if they were the same model.

A Personalization Example: What the Model Sees

Consider a fictional social platform that recommends posts. A user spends more time reading cooking content than sports content. The system can represent that behavior as numerical signals and use it to rank future recommendations.

The network does not know why the user lingered. It sees patterns in the available data and learns associations that help optimize a defined objective, such as predicting engagement.

That makes neural networks powerful, but it also reveals a limitation: a useful prediction is not necessarily a complete explanation. A long viewing time might indicate interest, confusion, or even that the user left the page open. The data and the training objective shape what the model can learn.

In my white paper, this example led to a larger question: when a system is optimized for personalization, how do we prevent it from collecting more information than it needs or reinforcing patterns that disadvantage users?

Accuracy Is Not the Only Measure of a Good Model

Neural networks can be difficult to interpret. A prediction may depend on many interacting weights, making it harder to explain why one recommendation appeared instead of another. This is often called the black-box problem.

Bias is another concern. If training data reflects unequal representation or historical patterns, a model may reproduce those patterns. Testing only aggregate accuracy can conceal problems experienced by particular groups.

I argued in my research that evaluation should include more than performance metrics. Developers should examine data quality, document intended uses, test for disparate outcomes where appropriate, and provide ways to investigate or challenge consequential automated decisions.

Explainable AI techniques can help communicate model behavior, but explanations should not be presented as guarantees that a complex system is fair or error-free.

Privacy by Design: Lessons From My GDPR Research

My white paper examined seven GDPR data-protection principles in the context of neural-network personalization: lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimization; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; and accountability.

Those principles translate into concrete engineering questions. What information is actually needed for the model? Why was it collected? How long should it be retained? Who can access it? Can the organization explain its processing and respond to user requests?

For a hypothetical recommendation platform, I proposed clearer privacy notices, stronger limits on secondary uses of data, defined retention periods, access controls, and regular audits. These are design and governance recommendations, not a claim that any particular platform implemented them.

I also explored approaches such as differential privacy, federated learning, and explainability. Each can address a specific concern, but none automatically makes a system compliant. Legal obligations depend on the processing context, and technical safeguards still require careful implementation and evaluation.

What This Research Changed About How I Think

The most valuable part of studying neural networks was moving beyond the idea that a model is successful simply because it produces a convincing result.

Working on the Treasure Hunt agent taught me to examine rewards, exploration, training stability, and whether behavior actually improved. Writing about personalization made me ask an additional set of questions about the data and people affected by a model.

Both experiences point to the same engineering habit: define what success means before declaring a system successful. A model can be technically functional while its training signals are misleading, its explanations are weak, or its data practices are inappropriate.

Neural networks are useful because they can learn complex patterns. Building them responsibly means understanding the calculations, questioning the objective, validating the results, and being transparent about the limits.
